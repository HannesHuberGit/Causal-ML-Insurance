Message: from the toy to our real setup. Double ML has two stages with two different jobs, and we chose a learner for each. The strip is the recipe from the "How Double ML works" slide, in the same notation, built one step per click. X now stands for all customer features (rating factors), not just age. Click 1, first stage, lapse model: predict lapse Y from the features, ℓ̂(X). The residual Ỹ = Y − ℓ̂(X) is the lapse the features cannot explain. Click 2, first stage, price model: predict price T from the features, m̂(X): the tariff. The residual T̃ = T − m̂(X) is the free price variation (discounts, loadings, tests). Both first stages are pure prediction, judged like any ML model on hold-out loss, and cross-fitted as before. Click 3, final stage: read the price effect from the residuals. In the toy it was one number, Ỹ ≈ θ̂·T̃. Now θ̂(X) may depend on the customer: different customers can react differently to price. How flexibly θ̂(X) may vary is the choice on the next slide. This is the causal step: there is no ground truth to score it against, so it rests on the assumptions in "What Double ML needs". Click 4, why LightGBM in the first stage: - Many rating factors: gradient-boosted trees learn non-linear shapes and interactions without us specifying them, unlike a GLM. - Categories and gaps: native handling of categorical features (also many levels) and missing values. - Fast: cross-fitting fits both first-stage models once per fold, so speed matters. - Only needs to predict well: the callback to the previous slide. The final-stage bias is a product of the two first-stage errors, so the first stage does not need to be perfect, only good. This is exactly what a strong prediction model delivers. Alternatives: GLM or Lasso (too rigid for many factors; its errors do not shrink and leave bias), random forests, neural networks (more tuning). Double ML allows any of them; LightGBM was the pragmatic default.
