200,000 simulated policies · age groups ±2.5 years

Message: age drives both our price and the customer's loyalty. The price effect only becomes visible when age is held fixed, and it is small next to the age effect. Builds. Start: all policies in grey, and the pale line is the raw trend across all ages: lapse falls as price rises. Pause and ask the room: "So should we raise prices to make customers more loyal?" Click 1: colour by age group. Within each narrow group (age ± 2.5 years) the tariff barely changes (about ±0.6%), so prices differ mainly by the random discount or loading. There, a higher price means more lapse, slope ≈ +1 pp per +1% price. The five fitted slopes scatter around 1 (sampling noise, plus a slight pull-down from the age range left within each group). Why the groups drift down from left to right: moving right means moving to older customers, who pay more (our tariff) AND lapse less (loyalty). The raw trend is that age effect, not a price effect. Click 2: the summary. All ages together, a higher price goes with lower lapse; at fixed age, a higher price means higher lapse. The difference is age, which moves both. Size of the two effects, if asked: holding price fixed, age 25 → 75 lowers lapse by 29 pp; holding age fixed, discounts and loadings (standard deviation 2%) typically move lapse by about ±2 pp. The effect we want is the small one. This matters on the closing slide: a model tuned for prediction pays most attention to the big signal. Every model from here on, LightGBM and Double ML alike, sees age. The question is how well each separates a small price effect from a large age effect. Only if asked: the raw slope across all ages is −0.86 pp per +1%. It equals the true +1.00 plus a confounding term Cov(price, age effect g) / Var(price) = −1.86.
