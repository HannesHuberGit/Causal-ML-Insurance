The question

A price change is an intervention, not a prediction

“What happens to lapse and margin if we raise prices by x%?” An accurate lapse model can still get this wrong.

Toy world with a known answer: +1% price → +1.0 pp lapse · 200,000 simulated policies · effect sizes exaggerated for visibility

Message: pricing asks a what-if (intervention); a lapse model answers "who lapses at which price" (prediction). - They differ because we set prices from the same factors that drive lapse. No builds. Pose the question, then promise: we test on a toy portfolio where the answer is known (footer). Toy world (known truth, so every method can be graded) - Age 25–75, one rating factor (in practice: all rating factors). - Tariff: +2.5% per 10 years of age (+13% from 25 to 75). - Plus discounts and loadings, SD 2%, symmetric: the only "experiment" in the data. - Lapse = g(age) + θ·T, θ = 1: +1% price → +1.0 pp lapse for everybody. Average lapse 26%. - 200,000 policies; effect sizes exaggerated, mechanisms as in real data. Symbols for later slides - Y lapse (0/1) · T log price vs age-25 base · X age (in practice all rating factors) · θ price effect, pp per +1%. - g(X) age effect · ℓ(X) = E[Y | X] lapse model · m(X) = E[T | X] price model (what the tariff predicts). - V = T − m(X) free price variation · Ỹ = Y − ℓ̂(X), T̃ = T − m̂(X) estimated residuals. - f̂ = LightGBM with price as a feature, estimating f(X, T) = E[Y | X, T].
