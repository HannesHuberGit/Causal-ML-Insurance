Same first stage in all three; only the last step differs

Message: the toy had one price effect for everyone. Real customers differ: young vs old, new vs long-standing, online vs agent. The first stage stays the same; the final stage decides how the effect may vary across customers. Builds, left to right: simple → flexible → flexible and reliable. Click 1, Linear (econml LinearDML). What the toy used: one price effect, or a linear formula in a few chosen features. Easy to explain and stable, with standard confidence intervals. Limitation: the effect can only vary in the shape we write down. Click 2, Boosted final stage. A LightGBM model learns the price effect as a function of the customer features from the first-stage residuals. We followed Causal Inference for the Brave and True (M. Facure, chapter on Debiased/Orthogonal ML). In its principled form this is the R-learner (Nie and Wager, 2021; in econml NonParamDML with a LightGBM final model): regress the lapse residual divided by the price residual on the features, weighted by the squared price residual. Strength: the most flexible final stage, any shape, any ML model. Weakness: the residuals are noisy, and where the price residual is small the model can chase that noise. There are no confidence intervals out of the box. Click 3, Causal forest (econml CausalForestDML), our choice. The forest grows its trees to separate customers whose price effect differs, and uses honest trees and built-in confidence intervals. Why this won: next slide. The honest trade-off: a segment-level effect is harder to explain than one number.
