Same first stage in all three · the causal forest performed best in our project

Message: real customers react differently to price, so θ̂(X) must be allowed to vary. Of three final stages, only the causal forest is flexible, resists noise and gives uncertainty ranges. Same first stage in all three. Builds - Start: the four criteria. Ask: "What do we need from the final stage?" - Click 1, Linear (econml LinearDML): what the toy used. - One effect, or a linear formula in a few chosen features. - Stable, standard CIs, easy to explain. Varies only in the shape we write down. - Click 2, Boosted final stage: - Source: Facure, Causal Inference for the Brave and True (Debiased/Orthogonal ML chapter). - Principled form: R-learner (Nie and Wager, 2021); econml NonParamDML with a LightGBM final model. Regress Ỹ / T̃ on the features, weighted by T̃². - Any shape; but same data picks splits and sets values → chases noise (early stopping helps only partly). - No CIs out of the box; explainable via usual boosting tools (partly). - Click 3, Causal forest (econml CausalForestDML), our choice: - Any shape, resists noise, confidence ranges per customer or segment. - Harder to explain than one number (partly): the price of flexibility. - Click 4, how it does it: - Splits on the effect: separates customers who react more to price from those who react less, not where lapse differs → "Flexible shape". - Honest trees: half A chooses the splits, half B measures the leaf effects. A chance split can't set its own effect → "Resists fitting noise". - Effect measured on data the split never saw → valid ranges (Wager and Athey, 2018; Athey, Tibshirani and Wager, 2019) → "Uncertainty ranges". Footer - Best of the three in our project. Part of the gain is likely honesty itself, not only flexibility. - "What every method needs" (convergence slide) still holds for all three. Takeaway of the deck: prediction models to predict; causal estimators, with explicit assumptions, to decide.
